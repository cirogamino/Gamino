# THE OFFICE — target spec

The end state, locked. `office.html` (isometric) is Phase 0 — deliberately the
cheapest thing that works, shipped so the data layer is real while the visual
layer catches up. This file is the north star it is climbing toward.

**The bar, in Ciro's words:** someone walks into the office, sees the screen, and
says *holy shit, he has real employees.* If it doesn't survive that reaction, it
isn't done.

---

## LOCKED DECISIONS

| Decision | Answer |
|---|---|
| Surfaces | All four — wall display, laptop, phone, and on camera for the show |
| The people | Photoreal, pre-rendered |
| Camera | Fixed cinematic angles |
| Budget | Browser-only, no new spend |

---

## THE ONE CONTRADICTION

Photoreal people and zero spend do not both fit. Ten consistent synthetic humans
have to be generated by something, and generation costs either money or credits.
There is no free path to photoreal humans — anyone who says otherwise is going to
hand back stock footage of strangers, which fails the believability bar the
moment someone looks twice.

Three honest ways out, in the order worth trying:

1. **Spend credits, not dollars.** The generation tools already connected to this
   account (Higgsfield and friends) can produce the character clips. That is not
   *new* spend — it is capacity already paid for. This is almost certainly the
   answer, and it needs one decision: how many credits the character library is
   worth. **Nothing gets generated until that number is set.**
2. **Photoreal room, people implied.** Real light, real glass, real reflections,
   ten live monitors, chairs pushed out, a coat over one back. No humans rendered
   at all. Genuinely free, and it clears the bar more often than people expect —
   the hardest thing to fake is simply absent. It reads as *the team just stepped
   out*, not as *these are fake people*.
3. **Stylized premium instead of photoreal.** Free, live, and never uncanny,
   because it doesn't enter the valley. But nobody mistakes it for a photograph,
   so it fails the specific reaction being aimed at.

Recommendation: **(1), with (2) built first as the plate underneath it.** Option 2
is not a consolation prize — it is literally the background layer that option 1
composites onto. Building it is never wasted work, and if the character library
disappoints, the fallback is already shipped and already good.

## THE ARCHITECTURE

The fixed camera is what makes all of this cheap. A locked angle means nothing
ever has to hold up from a direction nobody will look from — which is exactly how
film has always done it.

```
LAYER 4   Live HTML overlay — nameplates, status, run strip, drawer
LAYER 3   LIVE MONITOR SURFACES — real HTML, perspective-mapped onto each screen
LAYER 2   Character clips — one looping video per seat per state, fixed position
LAYER 1   The plate — the empty office at this camera angle
```

**Layer 3 is the whole trick.** Because the camera never moves, each monitor in
the plate occupies a fixed quadrilateral on screen forever. A single CSS
`matrix3d` per monitor maps a live HTML panel into that quad. The screens then
show the seat's *actual* current artifact, updating in real time, with correct
perspective — inside otherwise baked footage.

That is what converts "nice render" into "holy shit." A visitor watches a monitor
change while they are standing there. Baked footage cannot do that, and it is the
detail no one expects to be real, which is precisely why it lands.

### Why this satisfies all four surfaces from one build

| Surface | Why it works |
|---|---|
| Wall display | Video plus DOM. Runs forever on a cheap stick PC, no GPU. |
| Laptop | Same page. The overlay stays crisp because it is real text, not pixels. |
| Phone | Video scales; the overlay reflows. A real-time 3D scene would have died here — this doesn't. |
| On camera | Already cinematic by construction. The locked angle *is* the shot. |

A live 3D engine would have needed four different quality tiers and a streaming
bill. This needs one build.

## THE ASSET LIST

Small enough to be finishable, which is the point.

**Plates (2):** the open floor, and the war room. Empty. No people.

**Character clips (~22):** ten seats × two states — `working` (leaning in, typing,
occasional shift) and `idle` (back from the desk, phone out, feet up) — plus two
war-room group loops. Each 6–10 seconds, seamless, alpha-matted or shot against a
matching plate for clean compositing.

**Absent needs no asset.** No signal = composite nothing. Empty chair, dark
screen. The most important state is free, which is fitting, because it is the one
that has to be unmistakable.

**Consistency is the hard requirement.** The same ten faces every time, or the
illusion dies on the second viewing. Character sheets locked first, one per seat,
before a single clip is generated.

## PHASES

Each one ships and stands on its own. No phase depends on the next one existing.

**Phase 0 — done.** `office.html`. Isometric, live, honest. Establishes the data
contract every later phase reuses: `PLACE`, `ARTIFACT`, and the six states.

**Phase 1 — the live board.** `GET /board` on the Worker, `POST /board/:seat` for
wired seats. Until the data is live, a photoreal office is a screensaver. This is
the phase that actually matters and the one most likely to get skipped because it
isn't the fun part.

**Phase 2 — the plate.** Photoreal empty office at the locked angle, with live
perspective-mapped monitors. This is option (2) above, shipped standalone. First
moment it stops looking like software.

**Phase 3 — the people.** Character sheets, then clip library, then compositing.
The credit decision gates this phase and only this phase.

**Phase 4 — the cuts.** Two or three angles with slow drift, cutting on events:
hold on the floor, push to the war room when seats gather, snap to a desk that
just went red. Motion driven by what is actually happening is what separates this
from a screensaver.

---

## THE RULE THAT SURVIVES EVERY PHASE

**Idle and no-signal never look the same.** Feet-up means the seat reported an
empty queue and wants work. An empty chair means it went quiet and nobody knows
why. As the visuals get more convincing, this gets *more* important, not less —
a photoreal office that renders silence as a relaxed employee is a very expensive
way to lie to yourself.

Believability is the goal. Accuracy is the constraint. When they conflict,
accuracy wins, because the moment this thing shows something that isn't true it
stops being an instrument and becomes a prop.
