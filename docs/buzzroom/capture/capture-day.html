The building is still gutted — read this first

A splat is not a skeleton and not a model. It is millions of fuzzy coloured blobs that reproduce exactly what the camera saw. There is no wall to edit, no surface to grab, no wireframe underneath. Scan a stud-frame shell today and what you get back is a photoreal stud-frame shell — I cannot fill in walls that were never in front of the lens.

That does not mean today is wasted. It means shooting three different things, for three different reasons.

What to shoot, and when

Now · The outside do this today

The Greenfield Avenue face and the doors with the E are finished and untouched by the demolition. This is the show's headquarters shot and it is ready right now. Full instructions in The passes — outside below.

Now · The empty shell, as a measuring tape worth 15 min

Your instinct was half right. The shell will not be the finished look, but it does give me something I genuinely need: true dimensions and real architecture — exact footprint, ceiling height, where the windows and columns actually sit. I build the finished 3D room to those measurements instead of guessing, and it lines up with the real place when the walls go up. Bare studs are also easier to capture than a finished room: lots of texture, no mirrors, no glass.

Now · The “before” free content

Same footage doubles as the opening of the show. Episode one is an empty gutted box; by episode eight it is a working office. That transformation is the story, and it only exists if you shoot it before the walls close in. You cannot go back for it later.

Later · The real interior capture once walls are up

The photoreal room only exists once the room exists. Shoot it when it is finished, lit, and furnished — that is the one that becomes the Buzzroom.

Before you press record

The passes — inside

Slow, heel-to-toe. Each frame should overlap its neighbour by 70–80%. If you feel silly at how slow you're going, it's right.

Shooting 4K video and letting the app pull frames is easier than taking hundreds of stills. A slow 3–4 minute walk gives plenty.

What kills a capture

Moving fast

Motion blur is the number one cause of a mushy result — more than the software you pick.

Auto-exposure left on

The brightness shifts frame to frame and the solver cannot line the shots up.

Anything that moved

A person walking through, a door swinging, a screen that changed. Freeze the room.
